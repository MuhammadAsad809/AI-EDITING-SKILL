# Cutting Theory

## Choose the cut by intention

| Technique | Use when | Avoid when | Professional check |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hard cut | New information or energy should arrive immediately | The viewer needs a breath or spatial bridge | Does the new frame have a clear reason to appear now? |
| Match-on-action | Preserve motion across a change in angle | Motion direction or timing conflicts | Cut on the action, not after it finishes |
| Reaction cut | Transfer meaning to a face or audience response | Reaction is repetitive or tells the viewer what to feel | Does the reaction add information? |
| Smash cut | Create an intentional contrast or shock | The contrast is unprepared | Build the expectation before breaking it |
| Jump cut | Compress time or create nervous immediacy | Continuity is the selling point | Change framing, action, or intention enough to be legible |
| Graphic match | Link shapes, lines, color, or movement | The visual similarity is accidental | Hold the common graphic long enough to register |
| Parallel cut | Compare simultaneous actions or ideas | The events have no meaningful relationship | Maintain distinct screen geography and escalating stakes |

## Decision rules

Cut at the earliest frame where the current shot has delivered its purpose and the next shot can carry the viewer forward. For dialogue, cut on thought changes rather than every sentence. For action, cut just before or during the decisive movement so the next angle completes it. For emotion, hold until the viewer has time to read the face, then cut on a changed thought or breath.

A cut can be motivated by information, movement, gaze, sound, rhythm, graphic similarity, or emotional contrast. State the motivation in a plan. If no motivation exists, prefer a clean cut and revisit the underlying shot order.
